## 1. Autor (2015)
**Citation:** Autor, David H. 2015. "Why Are There Still So Many Jobs? The History and Future of Workplace Automation." *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 29(3): 3–30.
**DOI:** 10.1257/jep.29.3.3 — https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/109476 ; PDF: https://shapingwork.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/autor-2015-why-are-there-still-so-many-jobs-the-history-and-future-of-workplace-automation.pdf
**Findings:**
- Agriculture employed about 41% of the US workforce in 1900 and about 2% in 2000.
- ATMs rose from about 100,000 to 400,000 (1995–2010) while bank teller employment rose from about 500,000 to 550,000 (1980–2010); tellers per branch fell by more than a third (1988–2004) as urban branches rose by more than 40% (citing Bessen 2015).
- Middle-skill occupations fell from 60% of employment (1979) to 49% (2007) and 46% (2012).
**Status:** VERIFIED

## 2. Autor, Levy and Murnane (2003)
**Citation:** Autor, David H., Frank Levy, and Richard J. Murnane. 2003. "The Skill Content of Recent Technological Change: An Empirical Exploration." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 118(4): 1279–1333.
**DOI:** 10.1162/003355303322552801 — https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v118y2003i4p1279-1333..html (NBER WP 8337)
**Note:** Full title includes the subtitle "An Empirical Exploration".
**Findings:**
- Computer capital substitutes for routine cognitive and routine manual tasks and complements nonroutine analytic and interactive tasks (task data 1960–1998).
- Task shifts explain about 60% of the estimated relative demand shift favouring college labour, 1970–1998.
- Within-occupation task change accounts for almost half of that effect.
**Status:** VERIFIED

## 3. Hsieh and Moretti (2019)
**Citation:** Hsieh, Chang-Tai, and Enrico Moretti. 2019. "Housing Constraints and Spatial Misallocation." *American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics* 11(2): 1–39.
**DOI:** 10.1257/mac.20170388 — https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/mac.20170388 ; working PDF https://eml.berkeley.edu//~moretti/growth.pdf
**Findings (as published):**
- Housing constraints "lowered aggregate US growth by 36 percent from 1964 to 2009" (220 MSAs; counterfactual lowers regulation in New York, San Jose and San Francisco to the median city). The 36% is a reduction in the growth *rate*, not a 36% GDP-level loss.
- Imperfect mobility: 2009 US GDP 3.7% higher, about $3,685 more average wages per worker (~$0.53 trillion wage bill).
- Perfect mobility upper bound: 2009 GDP about 8.9% higher, about $8,775 per worker.
**WARNING — contested estimate:**
- Bryan Caplan (2021) identified, and the authors accepted, a compounding error: the stated growth-rate effects imply roughly +14% (imperfect mobility) and +36% (perfect mobility) GDP levels by 2009, not 3.7% / 8.9%. Source: https://www.betonit.ai/p/a-correction-on-housing-regulation (blog, not peer-reviewed).
- Greaney, Brian. 2026. "Housing Constraints and Spatial Misallocation: Comment." *AEJ: Macroeconomics* 18(2): 409–28. DOI 10.1257/mac.20230141. Reports that replication with the original code lowers output, traces this to code errors and dependence on the arbitrary population unit, and finds a corrected positive effect about two orders of magnitude smaller than reported. https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/mac.20230141 . Hsieh disputes the comment (https://www.betonit.ai/p/hsieh-replies-to-greaney).
- Recommendation: do not cite a single GDP-loss figure from Hsieh–Moretti without citing Greaney (2026); Glaeser and Gyourko's 0.7–2% range (item 5) is a more defensible magnitude.
**Status:** VERIFIED (citation); headline estimate CONTESTED in the peer-reviewed literature

## 4. Ganong and Shoag (2017)
**Citation:** Ganong, Peter, and Daniel Shoag. 2017. "Why Has Regional Income Convergence in the U.S. Declined?" *Journal of Urban Economics* 102: 76–90.
**DOI:** 10.1016/j.jue.2017.07.002 — https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v102y2017icp76-90.html (NBER WP 23609)
**Findings:**
- State incomes converged at about 1.8% per year from 1880 to 1980; the 1990–2010 rate was less than half the historical norm (working-paper version, https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/Ganong-Shoag.pdf).
- Decline coincides with disproportionate house-price growth in high-income places and a reversal of low-skill migration toward them; convergence has mostly stopped in highly regulated places and continues in less regulated ones (new panel measure of land-use regulation).
- Skill-biased technical change mechanically accounts for only about 0.18 of an observed 1.11 change in annual convergence rates. The paper does not assign a numeric share of the decline to regulation.
**Status:** VERIFIED

## 5. Glaeser and Gyourko (2018)
**Citation:** Glaeser, Edward, and Joseph Gyourko. 2018. "The Economic Implications of Housing Supply." *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 32(1): 3–30.
**DOI:** 10.1257/jep.32.1.3 — https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/jecper/v32y2018i1p3-30.html (NBER WP 23833)
**Findings:**
- Metro areas with median price above 1.25× minimum profitable production cost: 6.4% (1985), ~48% (2007), 15.4% (2013); the 11 metros above 1.25 in 2013 hold about 23% of the urban population.
- San Francisco 2013: median price ~$800,000 vs production cost ~$281,690 (ratio 2.84).
- Reallocation gains from deregulation estimated at about 0.7%–2% of GDP using empirical labour-demand elasticities (versus the much larger Hsieh–Moretti figure).
**Status:** VERIFIED

## 6. Şahin, Song, Topa and Violante (2014)
**Citation:** Şahin, Ayşegül, Joseph Song, Giorgio Topa, and Giovanni L. Violante. 2014. "Mismatch Unemployment." *American Economic Review* 104(11): 3529–64.
**DOI:** 10.1257/aer.104.11.3529 — https://www.aeaweb.org/issues/352 ; NBER WP 18265 https://www.nber.org/papers/w18265
**Findings:**
- Industry and occupation mismatch explains at most one-third of the observed rise in US unemployment during the Great Recession (JOLTS and HWOL data).
- Geographic mismatch plays no apparent role.
- The share explained by occupational mismatch rises with education.
**Status:** VERIFIED

## 7. Jacobson, LaLonde and Sullivan (1993)
**Citation:** Jacobson, Louis S., Robert J. LaLonde, and Daniel G. Sullivan. 1993. "Earnings Losses of Displaced Workers." *American Economic Review* 83(4): 685–709.
**DOI:** none displayed (JSTOR) — https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v83y1993i4p685-709.html
**Findings:**
- High-tenure workers separating from distressed firms suffer long-term losses averaging 25% of pre-displacement earnings per year.
- Earnings start falling about three years before separation; mass-layoff separators are about $1,500 per quarter below expected five years after (≈$1,200 against same-firm stayers), with little recovery after year three.
- Sample: 5% of Pennsylvania workers, quarterly earnings 1974–1986, separations 1980–1986, 9,568 separators.
**Status:** VERIFIED

## 8. Davis and von Wachter (2011)
**Citation:** Davis, Steven J., and Till von Wachter. 2011. "Recessions and the Costs of Job Loss." *Brookings Papers on Economic Activity* 43(2) (Fall): 1–72.
**DOI:** none displayed for journal version (NBER WP 17638, DOI 10.3386/w17638) — https://www.brookings.edu/articles/recessions-and-the-costs-of-job-loss/ ; https://muse.jhu.edu/article/475017
**Findings:**
- Men displaced in mass layoffs lose, in present value (5% discount rate, 20 years), about 1.4 years of pre-displacement earnings when national unemployment is below 6%.
- Losses double to about 2.8 years when unemployment exceeds 8%.
- A Mortensen–Pissarides model with on-the-job search produces losses about one-fourth as large as observed, with little cyclical variation (Social Security records 1974–2008).
**Status:** VERIFIED

## 10. Goldin, C. — "Babies and the Macroeconomy"
**Working paper:** Goldin, Claudia. 2024. "Babies and the Macroeconomy." NBER Working Paper 33311, December 2024. https://ideas.repec.org/n/nep-dem/2025-01-06.html
**Journal article:** Goldin, Claudia. 2025. "Babies and the Macroeconomy." *Economica* 92(367): 675–700 (July). DOI 10.1111/ecca.12585 — https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/econom/v92y2025i367p675-700.html ; published PDF on author page.
**Note:** It is an Economica original article, not an "Economica lecture". The material was delivered as the European Central Bank Jean Monnet Lecture, 19 September 2024 (author page; acknowledgments). Cite it as the Economica article. (Goldin's site shows a stale "vol. 93, pp. 1–26" early-view listing; RePEc's 92(367): 675–700 is the issue pagination.)
**Findings:**
- Group 1 (Denmark, France, Germany, Sweden, UK, USA) all had TFR below 2 by about 1980; Group 2 (Greece, Italy, Japan, Korea, Portugal, Spain) fell below 2 by the mid-1980s and converged to about 1.3 by the late 1990s ("lowest low").
- 2019 TFR: Japan 1.36, Italy 1.27, versus 1.7 in Sweden and Denmark. Log GDP per capita growth per decade: Japan 0.800 (1960s), Korea 0.745 (1970s), versus roughly 0.07–0.24 for Group 1.
- Women's minus men's daily household and care hours: Japan 3.1, Italy 3.0, Sweden 0.8, Denmark 0.9; argument: rapid growth outpaces change in gender norms, men cling to patriarchal traditions, and the resulting conflict depresses fertility.
**Status:** VERIFIED

## 11. Dettling and Kearney (2014)
**Citation:** Dettling, Lisa J., and Melissa S. Kearney. 2014. "House Prices and Birth Rates: The Impact of the Real Estate Market on the Decision to Have a Baby." *Journal of Public Economics* 110: 82–100.
**DOI:** 10.1016/j.jpubeco.2013.09.009 — https://www.econ.umd.edu/publication/house-prices-and-birth-rates-impact-real-estate-market-decision-have-baby ; NBER WP 17485
**Findings (stated as % per $10,000 house-price increase, not as elasticities):**
- +$10,000 in MSA house prices raises fertility among owners by about 5%.
- The same increase lowers fertility among non-owners by about 2.4%.
- Net effect at the mean homeownership rate is about +0.8%; house-price changes have a larger effect on current births than unemployment changes.
**Status:** VERIFIED

## 12. Kearney, Levine and Pardue (2022)
**Citation:** Kearney, Melissa S., Phillip B. Levine, and Luke Pardue. 2022. "The Puzzle of Falling US Birth Rates since the Great Recession." *Journal of Economic Perspectives* 36(1): 151–76.
**DOI:** 10.1257/jep.36.1.151 — https://econ.umd.edu/sites/www.econ.umd.edu/files/pubs/jep.36.1.151.pdf ; NBER WP 29286
**Findings:**
- Births per 1,000 women aged 15–44 fell from 69.1 (2007) to 58.3 (2019) and 55.8 (2020), about a 20% fall, after staying near 65–70 from 1980 to 2007.
- Ten economic and policy variables jointly explain only about 6.2% of the 2007–2018 decline; the unemployment spike implied ~3.5% fewer births 2008–2011 versus an actual 7.2% fall.
- The decline is within demographic groups (12.8 per 1,000 holding population shares fixed); the authors attribute it to shifting priorities across cohorts.
**Status:** VERIFIED

## 13. Moretti (2010)
**Citation:** Moretti, Enrico. 2010. "Local Multipliers." *American Economic Review: Papers & Proceedings* 100(2): 373–77.
**DOI:** 10.1257/aer.100.2.373 — https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:aea:aecrev:v:100:y:2010:i:2:p:373-77 ; author PDF https://eml.berkeley.edu/~moretti/multipliers.pdf (preprint paginated 1–7; use 373–77)
**Findings:**
- Each additional manufacturing (tradable) job creates 1.59 (≈1.6) nontradable jobs in the same city (IV, SE 0.26).
- Skilled tradable job: 2.52 local jobs; unskilled: 1.04 (both imprecise).
- High-tech (machinery and computing, electrical machinery, professional equipment): 4.9 local jobs. Use 4.9, not "~5" without qualification.
**Status:** VERIFIED

## 14. Autor, Dorn and Hanson (2013)
**Citation:** Autor, David H., David Dorn, and Gordon H. Hanson. 2013. "The China Syndrome: Local Labor Market Effects of Import Competition in the United States." *American Economic Review* 103(6): 2121–68.
**DOI:** 10.1257/aer.103.6.2121 — https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/95952 ; PDF https://www.ddorn.net/papers/Autor-Dorn-Hanson-ChinaSyndrome.pdf
**Findings:**
- +$1,000 per worker in import exposure per decade reduces manufacturing employment per working-age population by 0.596 percentage points; mean weekly earnings fall 0.76 log points, concentrated outside manufacturing.
- Rising Chinese exposure explains 33% (1990–2000), 55% (2000–2007) and 44% (1990–2007) of the manufacturing employment decline in the benchmark; supply-driven conservative shares 16%, 26%, 21%. The abstract's rounded summary is about one-quarter.
- Evidence on slow adjustment: transfers rise $58 per capita per $1,000 of exposure, and there is no robust population response ("the effect of trade exposure shocks on population flows is small").
**Status:** VERIFIED

## 15. Baumol (1967)
**Citation:** Baumol, William J. 1967. "Macroeconomics of Unbalanced Growth: The Anatomy of Urban Crisis." *American Economic Review* 57(3): 415–26.
**DOI:** none (JSTOR-era). Bibliographic details confirmed from the Bristol growth references page, Bank of England WP (2000), Böckler (2021) and Tabarrok reference lists; no publisher or RePEc landing page for the original article was reachable.
**Findings (theoretical; no empirical estimates):**
- Two-sector model: a "progressive" sector with rising labour productivity and a "stagnant" sector with little or none.
- Common wages force rising relative costs in the stagnant sector (the "cost disease").
- If resources shift toward stagnant sectors, aggregate productivity growth falls toward the stagnant sector's rate (an implication that holds when stagnant outputs are final goods, per later critiques).
**Status:** VERIFIED (via multiple concordant secondary reference lists; full title includes subtitle "The Anatomy of Urban Crisis")
