# I. Economists: verified sources for the digital-layer / conversion-friction thesis

Fields that could not be confirmed are marked **[unverified]**. Quotes are under 15 words and taken from fetched text. Page numbers come from the fetched text and are flagged where they were inferred.

Status key:
- **VERIFIED**: core citation confirmed (author, year, title, venue).
- **PARTIAL**: work exists and core details confirmed, but one or more fields (pages, DOI, volume, exact date) are unconfirmed or conflict between sources.
- **NOT VERIFIED**: could not be confirmed.

Thesis shorthand used in the relevance notes: (T1) gen-AI mainly automates and augments the digital layer; (T2) the pre-1995 physical economy is largely unchanged, but value and power are more lopsided; (T3) the physical economy has large unmet demand; (T4) the binding constraint is conversion friction (credentials, mobility, geography), which policy can remove.

## 1. Amartya Sen

### 1a. Development as Freedom (1999): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Sen, A. (1999). *Development as Freedom*. New York: Alfred A. Knopf. UK edition: Oxford: Oxford University Press, 1999 (ISBN 9780198297581); 2nd ed. OUP 2001 (ISBN 9780192893307).
- **URL:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Development_as_Freedom (publication data); text consulted at https://raggeduniversity.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/1_x_senDevelopmentasFreedom-_compressed.pdf
- **Argument:** Development is the expansion of substantive freedoms, and freedom is both its end and its main means. Sen names five instrumental freedoms: political freedoms, economic facilities, social opportunities, transparency guarantees and protective security. In ch. 1 (pp. 20–21; page numbers inferred from running headers) he uses European unemployment of 10–12 per cent to argue that unemployment "is not merely a deficiency of income that can be made up through transfers." It is also a source of "far-reaching debilitating effects on individual freedom, initiative, and skills." In ch. 1 (pp. 28–30) he treats freedom to enter labour markets and to change employers as a constitutive freedom, citing the end of bonded labour.
- **Relevance:** **Supports T4 normatively.** On Sen's account, a displaced worker who cannot move into available physical-economy work loses capabilities, and an income transfer does not restore them. This supports framing conversion friction as a deprivation of freedom and not only an allocative inefficiency. It also implies that UBI-type compensation for AI displacement is insufficient on its own.

### 1b. Employment, Technology and Development (1975): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Sen, A. (1975). *Employment, Technology and Development: A Study Prepared for the International Labour Office within the Framework of the World Employment Programme*. Foreword by L. Emmerij. Oxford: Clarendon Press.
- **URL:** https://vufind.lboro.ac.uk/Record/147782
- **Argument:** Only the catalogue record was retrieved. The subtitle confirms it is an ILO World Employment Programme study of how technique choice relates to employment in developing economies. **No content-level claim is verified**, so cite it for framing only, or consult the text before attributing a specific argument.
- **Relevance:** Potentially **supports T1/T4** as an early statement that technology choice is a policy variable for employment. Unconfirmed at content level.

### 1c. "Inequality, Unemployment and Contemporary Europe" (1997): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Sen, A. (1997). "Inequality, unemployment and contemporary Europe." *International Labour Review*, 136(2), Summer, 155–170. (The ILO PDF filename gives 155–172; the text runs to p. 170.) Also issued as LSE STICERD DEDPS 07.
- **URL:** https://webapps.ilo.org/public/libdoc/ilo/P/09602/09602(1997-136-2)155-172.pdf ; LSE: https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/6687
- **Argument:** Sen lists the penalties of unemployment that income measures miss (pp. 161–165): "wastage of productive power" (p. 161) and fiscal burden; loss of freedom and social exclusion; skill loss, since people "unlearn" by "not doing" (p. 161); psychological harm and suicide (pp. 161–162); ill-health and mortality (p. 162); "motivational decline and resignation" (pp. 162–163); damage to family relations (p. 163); ethnic and gender tensions (pp. 163–164); erosion of social values (p. 164); and organisational inflexibility, because fear of job loss makes workers resist new technology (pp. 164–165).
- **Relevance:** **Strongly supports T4** and the policy case for fast conversion. Skill atrophy and motivational decline mean that the costs of friction compound over time, so delayed conversion is worse than its income loss suggests. The point on organisational inflexibility also matters: high displacement risk may itself slow AI diffusion.

## 2. Kaushik Basu (former Chief Economist, World Bank; former Chief Economic Adviser, India)

### 2a. The Republic of Beliefs (2018): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Basu, K. (2018). *The Republic of Beliefs: A New Approach to Law and Economics*. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. 264 pp. ISBN 9780691177687 (paperback listed under 9780691210049).
- **URL:** https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691177687/the-republic-of-beliefs
- **Argument:** Basu builds a game-theoretic "focal point" approach to law. A law changes behaviour only by shifting beliefs about what others, including officials, will do, so laws that are written down often go unenforced. His examples include asymmetric bribery law and food vouchers.
- **Relevance:** **Qualifies T4.** Removing a credential or licensing barrier on paper does not guarantee conversion if employers, regulators and workers do not expect the new rule to be honoured. Policy design has to coordinate expectations as well as change statute.

### 2b. Beyond the Invisible Hand (2011): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Basu, K. (2011). *Beyond the Invisible Hand: Groundwork for a New Economics*. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. 273 pp.
- **URL:** https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2011/11/bookrevs.pdf (Monthly Labor Review review); https://philpapers.org/rec/BASBTI
- **Argument:** Basu holds that mainstream economics misreads Smith's invisible hand. Norms and culture shape behaviour, and the principle of free contract must sometimes be limited because of coercion that arises from unequal power. The final chapters address global poverty and inequality.
- **Relevance:** **Supports T2.** It offers a framework for treating lopsided power as a structural feature that market exchange does not self-correct.

### 2c. Basu on AI, labour and inequality (Project Syndicate): VERIFIED (texts paywalled; summaries only)
- **(i)** Basu, K. (2023, 1 June). "Governing the Unknown." *Project Syndicate*. https://www.project-syndicate.org/commentary/ai-technological-change-how-to-regulate-and-manage-by-kaushik-basu-2023-05 . He argues that AI raises urgent questions about work, and that technology is outpacing policymakers while "inequality is rising."
- **(ii)** Basu, K. (2026, 18 June). "The Promise and Peril of AGI." *Project Syndicate*. https://www.project-syndicate.org/commentary/agi-could-liberate-everyone-or-create-new-aristocracy-by-kaushik-basu-2026-06 . AGI could greatly raise living standards, but without broad ownership it could collapse labour demand and create a new aristocracy. He proposes "universal basic shares" (from the page description; the body is paywalled).
- **(iii)** Basu, K. (2026, 24 Aug). "The Age of Gated Recessions." *Project Syndicate*. https://www.project-syndicate.org/commentary/robust-aggregate-indicators-conceal-widening-economic-divide-by-kaushik-basu-2026-08 . Aggregate indicators hide a widening divide in which some workers and regions are in recession-like conditions during a boom, and AI may deepen it. He opens with a BLS month showing −23,000 jobs alongside unemployment falling to 4.1%.
- Also found but not read: "How to Stop the AI-pocalypse" (Sept 2026).
- **Relevance:** (iii) **strongly supports T2.** "Gated recession" is close to the lopsidedness claim: aggregate stability coexists with concentrated distress. (ii) **qualifies T1/T3.** Basu treats AGI as potentially economy-wide, and he answers with ownership rather than conversion, which is an alternative policy lever to T4.

## 3. Abhijit Banerjee & Esther Duflo: Good Economics for Hard Times (2019): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Banerjee, A. V., & Duflo, E. (2019). *Good Economics for Hard Times: Better Answers to Our Biggest Problems*. New York: PublicAffairs (Indian edition: Juggernaut, 2019). ISBN 9781610399500 (PublicAffairs); 9781541762879 (paperback).
- **Chapters (from the TOC):** 1 MEGA: Make Economics Great Again; **2 From the Mouth of the Shark** (migration); **3 The Pains from Trade**; 4 Likes, Wants, and Needs; 5 The End of Growth?; 6 In Hot Water; **7 Player Piano** (automation); 8 Legit.gov; 9 Cash and Care; Conclusion. (Page numbers could not be reliably matched to chapters from the scanned TOC.)
- **URLs:** TOC: https://obalkyknih.cz/file/toc/505748/pdf ; excerpt of ch. 2: https://www.downtoearth.org.in/amp/story/economy/good-economics-from-hard-times-no-one-leaves-home-unless--67335 ; MIT News: https://news.mit.edu/2019/good-economics-hard-times-1112
- **Argument, ch. 2 (migration/immobility):** The view that migration lowers wages is "simple, seductive, and wrong." The real puzzle is why so many people do not move despite large wage gaps. Examples: poor families stay out of Delhi despite large urban wage premia, and fewer than 350,000 Greeks emigrated in 2010–15 despite 27% unemployment. People tend to leave only when daily life collapses (Nepal's Maoist insurgency); bad harvests did not push them out because they could not afford to move.
- **Argument, ch. 3 and 7 (trade, automation):** Banerjee (MIT News): "Ordinary people like to stay in place." Displaced workers do not reallocate as models assume, and that undermines the standard case for trade and automation gains. The authors favour stronger Trade Adjustment Assistance and subsidies to keep affected firms and older workers in place. They argue that labour-intensive, place-bound public services such as education and elder care are "unlikely to be either replaced by technology" or outsourced. They are sceptical of UBI. A detailed account of ch. 7's policy content (for example, a robot tax) was **not verified** from fetched sources.
- **Relevance:** **Strongly supports T4 (mobility friction) and T3 (place-bound care demand).** It also **qualifies T4**: Banerjee and Duflo suggest immobility partly reflects preferences and attachment to place, not only removable barriers. That implies bringing jobs to people (place-based policy) as well as removing barriers to moving people.

## 4. Raghuram Rajan (former Governor, Reserve Bank of India; former IMF Chief Economist)

### 4a. The Third Pillar (2019): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Rajan, R. (2019). *The Third Pillar: How Markets and the State Leave the Community Behind*. New York: Penguin Press. 464 pp. ISBN 9780525558316.
- **URL:** https://www.imf.org/en/publications/fandd/issues/2019/09/book-review-of-the-third-pillar-by-raghuram-rajan (Loungani review, F&D Sept 2019)
- **Argument:** The community, a geographically bounded social group, is a third pillar alongside markets and the state. Society works best when the three are "appropriately balanced." Trade and technology shocks have hollowed out communities, and Rajan proposes devolving power to the local level so that dislocation is met with local responses. He also warns that communities can leave people "trapped by the past." He expects that as machines produce more goods, work will "center once again around inter-personal relationships."
- **Relevance:** **Supports T2 and T3** (physical, local and relational work as the future locus of employment) and **supports T4's geographic dimension.** It **qualifies T4** in arguing that rebuilding local economies is the remedy, not only mobility.

### 4b. Breaking the Mould (2023), with Rohit Lamba: VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Rajan, R. G., & Lamba, R. (2023). *Breaking the Mould: Reimagining India's Economic Future*. Gurugram: Penguin Random House India (published 6 Dec 2023).
- **URL:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breaking_the_Mould ; review: Rahman, A. (2024, 10 Jan), *The India Forum*, https://www.theindiaforum.in/book-reviews/india-economic-crossroads-how-does-it-go-forward
- **Argument:** Manufacturing-led growth is less viable for India. India should go from agriculture to high-end services (designing chips and phones rather than assembling them), invest heavily in human capital, and decentralise governance. The book criticises Make in India. The reviewer objects that most Indian workers' skills do not suit a service economy, leaving a "broken bridge of opportunity" between high-value outsourcing and informal work.
- **Relevance:** **Contradicts T1 in its Indian application.** Rajan and Lamba bet India's future on the digital/services layer that gen-AI most directly targets. The reviewer's "broken bridge" point **supports T4**, because the gap is a credential and skill conversion problem.

### 4c. Rajan on AI and jobs (2026): PARTIAL (secondary reports; PS title unverified)
- **(i)** Rajan, Project Syndicate commentary, c. March 2026 (title **[unverified]**), as reported by *Free Press Journal* (16 Mar 2026): https://www.freepressjournal.in/amp/tech/former-rbi-governor-raghuram-rajan-says-ai-job-apocalypse-may-be-exaggerated-adoption-politics-market-forces-will-decide-impact , and Storyboard18: https://www.storyboard18.com/brand-makers/will-ai-wipe-out-white-collar-jobs-raghuram-rajan-says-not-so-fast-92282.htm . Fears of rapid white-collar job loss may be exaggerated, since adoption outside software will be slow (automated telephone exchanges took decades). He sets out two scenarios. In the first, a few AI platforms charge high prices, firms cut white-collar staff, and displaced workers crowd into retail and hospitality, which depresses wages there. In the second, competing providers keep prices low and gains spread. Competition policy, AI pricing rules or excess-profit taxes could tilt the outcome.
- **(ii)** Bloomberg interview, 27 Feb 2026 (reported by letsdatascience.com): https://letsdatascience.com/news/rajan-warns-ai-disrupts-indias-software-sector-c0b0ae67 . AI will disrupt India's services sector, with software firms most exposed, but the "doomsday scenario" is overstated.
- **Relevance:** (i) **directly supports T1 and T2** (white-collar and software displacement; market concentration decides the distribution). His crowding scenario is **a direct warning against an uncritical T3/T4**: displaced white-collar workers moving into low-wage physical services could depress wages there unless the physical sectors they enter have real unmet demand and productivity. The paper should separate high-demand, credential-gated physical work from low-barrier, low-wage services.

## 5. Anton Korinek & Joseph Stiglitz / Korinek & Suh

### 5a. Korinek & Stiglitz: PARTIAL (year conflict)
- **Citation:** Korinek, A., & Stiglitz, J. E. (2019). "Artificial Intelligence and Its Implications for Income Distribution and Unemployment." In A. Agrawal, J. Gans & A. Goldfarb (Eds.), *The Economics of Artificial Intelligence: An Agenda* (ch. 14, pp. 349–390). Chicago: University of Chicago Press / NBER. ISBN 9780226613338. Earlier version: NBER WP 24174 (Dec 2017).
- **Date conflict:** NBER's page gives a 2018 citation but a May 2019 publication date (© 2019). IDEAS lists 2018. Most citations of the printed volume use 2019. **DOI [unverified].**
- **URL:** https://www.nber.org/books-and-chapters/economics-artificial-intelligence-agenda/artificial-intelligence-and-its-implications-income-distribution-and-unemployment ; https://ideas.repec.org/h/nbr/nberch/14018.html
- **Argument:** Worker-replacing technology generates inequality through two channels: innovator rents and factor-price redistribution. Technological change can be a Pareto improvement if losers are compensated through non-distortionary taxation, even in a "singularity." Unemployment can arise through efficiency-wage effects and as a transitional phenomenon.
- **Relevance:** **Supports T2** (distribution of gains as the central issue) and **T4** (transitional unemployment as a friction policy can address). **Qualifies T1:** the model applies to all worker-replacing technology, not only digital work.

### 5b. Korinek & Suh (2024): PARTIAL (DOI unverified)
- **Citation:** Korinek, A., & Suh, D. (2024). "Scenarios for the Transition to AGI." NBER Working Paper No. 32255. Also CEPR DP 18928; arXiv:2403.12107. **DOI [unverified].**
- **URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w32255 ; https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/32255.html
- **Argument:** Work is modelled as tasks of varying complexity. If automation is slow there is always work left for humans and wages can rise indefinitely. If human task complexity is bounded and full automation arrives, wages collapse. Wages can fall earlier if automation outpaces capital accumulation. Non-reproducible scarce factors can bottleneck growth.
- **Relevance:** **Qualifies or contradicts T1–T3 in the AGI limit.** If AI extends to physical tasks through robotics, the "unchanged physical economy" ceases to be a refuge. The paper should state T1 as a medium-run claim conditional on slow automation of physical tasks, which is Korinek and Suh's "always work left for humans" branch. Their scarce-factor bottleneck is compatible with T3 if physical capacity (land, housing, trades) is the scarce factor.

## 6. Dani Rodrik

### 6a. "Prospects for global economic convergence under new technologies" (2022): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Rodrik, D. (2022). "Prospects for global economic convergence under new technologies." In Z. Qureshi (Ed.), *An Inclusive Future? Technology, New Dynamics, and Policy Challenges* (pp. 65–82). Washington, DC: Brookings Institution.
- **URL:** https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/prospects-global-economic-convergence-under-new-technologies ; PDF: https://drodrik.scholars.harvard.edu/resource/pdf-151
- **Argument:** Recent developing-country growth occurred despite the absence of industrialisation, not because of it (p. 66). Manufacturing was an "escalator": it converged on frontier productivity, absorbed low-educated labour and could export (p. 67). Services-led growth is self-limiting (p. 67). Innovation in rich countries is predominantly labour-saving, and developing countries import that bias, including through value chains (pp. 70–72). The result is a "triple whammy" (pp. 74–75). Bangladesh's garment automation shows competitiveness shifting to scarce factors (p. 78). His remedy is a "good-jobs" strategy aimed at SMEs and informal productivity, plus "appropriate technology" as a global public good (pp. 79–80).
- **Relevance:** **Supports T2** (labour-saving bias concentrates gains) and **qualifies T1.** Labour-saving technical change has been hitting physical manufacturing employment for decades, before gen-AI. The physical economy is therefore "unchanged" in output but not in labour intensity, and the paper should make that distinction explicit.

### 6b. Rodrik & Sabel, "Building a Good Jobs Economy": PARTIAL
- **Citation verified:** Rodrik, D., & Sabel, C. (2020). "Building a Good Jobs Economy." HKS Faculty Research Working Paper RWP20-001, January 2020.
- **Book version [unverified]:** widely cited as a chapter in Allen, D., Benkler, Y., Downey, L., Henderson, R., & Simons, J. (Eds.) (2022), *A Political Economy of Justice* (University of Chicago Press). The volume itself was verified (UChicago 2022, 400 pp.), but the chapter's inclusion and page range were not.
- **URL:** https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/building-good-jobs-economy
- **Argument:** Globalisation and technology create "productive/technological dualism." An advanced, knowledge-intensive metropolitan sector sits beside less productive industries and communities that neither drive innovation nor gain from it, in rich and poor countries alike.
- **Relevance:** **Strongly supports T2.** Dualism is the lopsidedness claim stated in sectoral and spatial terms.

### 6c. Rodrik & Stantcheva (2021): PARTIAL (vol/issue/pages/DOI unverified)
- **Citation:** Rodrik, D., & Stantcheva, S. (2021). "Fixing capitalism's good jobs problem." *Oxford Review of Economic Policy*, 37(4) **[issue inferred from the special issue; volume, issue, pages and DOI not confirmed on a fetched page]**.
- **URL:** https://drodrik.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/fixing-capitalism%E2%80%99s-good-jobs-problem ; PDF: https://stantcheva.scholars.harvard.edu/resource/fixingcapitalismsgoodjobsproblemrodrikstantcheva2021pdf
- **Argument:** Education, training, progressive taxes and social insurance do not by themselves fix polarisation. A productive-side strategy is needed, with four parts: employer-linked active labour market policies, industrial and regional policies that create good jobs, innovation policy favouring labour-friendly technology, and international rules that protect domestic standards. "Good jobs cannot be generated by fiat." They cite deepening "spatial divides" and argue that the direction of technology "cannot be left to firms and innovators alone."
- **Relevance:** **Supports T2 and T4**, and **qualifies T4.** Rodrik and Stantcheva warn that supply-side retraining without employer linkage underperforms, which matches the Card–Kluve–Weber and Katz et al. evidence in entry 24.

## 7. Mariana Mazzucato

### 7a. The Entrepreneurial State (2013): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Mazzucato, M. (2013). *The Entrepreneurial State: Debunking Public vs. Private Sector Myths*. London: Anthem Press. 261 pp. Revised ed. 2015.
- **URL:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Entrepreneurial_State ; https://blog.anthempress.com/2013/07/19/anthem-book-spotlight-the-entrepreneurial-state/
- **Argument:** The state has been the "lead risk taker" in innovation. Technologies in Apple's smartphone (GPS, touch screen, voice recognition) came from public funding.
- **Relevance:** **Supports T2.** The digital layer was built on publicly funded technology while its returns were privatised, which bears on who should share AI gains.

### 7b. Mission Economy (2021): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Mazzucato, M. (2021). *Mission Economy: A Moonshot Guide to Changing Capitalism*. London: Allen Lane, 272 pp. (US: Harper Business).

- **Argument:** Organise policy around missions modelled on the moon landing. Public and private actors share risks and rewards, and the state rebuilds its capacity to direct innovation toward concrete problems.
- **Relevance:** **Supports T3/T4 as a policy template.** Housing, energy transition and care can be framed as missions that create physical-economy demand and the conversion pathways to meet it.

## 8. Erik Brynjolfsson (2022): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Brynjolfsson, E. (2022). "The Turing Trap: The Promise & Peril of Human-Like Artificial Intelligence." *Dædalus*, 151(2), 272–287. https://doi.org/10.1162/DAED_a_01915
- **URL:** https://www.amacad.org/publication/daedalus/turing-trap-promise-peril-human-artificial-intelligence ; PDF: https://www.amacad.org/sites/default/files/publication/downloads/Daedalus_Sp22_19_Brynjolfsson.pdf
- **Argument:** Human-like AI that substitutes for workers creates less value over time than AI that augments them. Technologists, managers and policymakers are all biased toward automation, and the tax code taxes labour more heavily than capital. When AI replicates human work, workers lose economic and political bargaining power. The Turing Trap is "an equilibrium in which those without power have no way to improve their outcomes."
- **Relevance:** **Supports T2** (power concentration) and supports a policy lever adjacent to T4 (rebalancing labour and capital taxation). It **qualifies T1** by treating the automation/augmentation mix as a choice, not a fixed property of the digital layer.

## 9. David Autor (2024): PARTIAL (DOI unverified)
- **Citation:** Autor, D. (2024). "Applying AI to Rebuild Middle Class Jobs." NBER Working Paper No. 32140, February 2024. Popular version: "How AI Could Help Rebuild the Middle Class," *Noema*. **DOI [unverified]**; URL as given in the paper.
- **URL:** https://www.nber.org/papers/w32140 ; PDF: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32140/w32140.pdf
- **Sections:** From Artisanal to Mass Expertise; From Mass Expertise to Elite Expertise in the Information Age; Complementing Judgement; Substitution versus Complementarity; The Twilight of Expertise?; A Scenario, Not a Forecast.
- **Argument:** Computerisation concentrated decision-making in elite experts. AI could let workers with foundational training make higher-stakes expert decisions. - **Relevance:** **Strongly supports T4, especially the credentials component.** Autor's own mechanism has institutions (scope-of-practice rules and certification) as the binding constraint on converting AI capability into middle-class work. It also supports T3 (health care) and shows how AI can lower the credential barrier to physical-economy roles.

## 10. Carl Benedikt Frey; Frey & Osborne; Arntz, Gregory & Zierahn

### 10a. The Technology Trap (2019): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Frey, C. B. (2019). *The Technology Trap: Capital, Labor, and Power in the Age of Automation*. Princeton, NJ: Princeton University Press. xiv + 465 pp. ISBN 978-0-691-17279-8.
- **URL:** https://eh.net/book_reviews/the-technology-trap-capital-labor-and-power-in-the-age-of-automation/ (Field review, EH.Net, Aug 2019)
- **Argument:** Frey distinguishes labour-replacing from labour-enabling technology. Early industrialisation produced an "Engels' pause" of stagnant wages and rising inequality before broad gains. The rise in inequality since the 1980s reflects a return to replacing technology. Losers rationally resist, and politics decides adoption.
- **Relevance:** **Supports T2.** It also **qualifies T4**: if transitions are mismanaged, resistance from losers can block diffusion, so conversion policy is a precondition for AI adoption as well as a welfare measure.

### 10b. Frey & Osborne (2017): PARTIAL (DOI unverified)
- **Citation:** Frey, C. B., & Osborne, M. A. (2017). "The future of employment: How susceptible are jobs to computerisation?" *Technological Forecasting and Social Change*, 114, 254–280. (ScienceDirect PII S0040162516302244; **DOI [unverified]**.) Working paper: Oxford Martin School, 1 Sept 2013.
- **URL:** https://www.inet.ox.ac.uk/publications/the-future-of-employment-how-susceptible-are-jobs-to-computerisation ; https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/publications/the-future-of-employment
- **Finding:** Using a Gaussian process classifier on 702 detailed occupations, they estimate "about 47 per cent of total US employment is at risk." Computerisation probability falls with wages and educational attainment.
- **Relevance:** **Contradicts T1 as originally framed.** It predicted that pre-gen-AI automation risk would be concentrated in low-wage routine work, including physical occupations. Gen-AI exposure studies (e.g., IMF 2024, entry 17) show the reverse gradient. That reversal is itself evidence for T1 and should be stated as such.

### 10c. Arntz, Gregory & Zierahn (2016): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Arntz, M., Gregory, T., & Zierahn, U. (2016). *The Risk of Automation for Jobs in OECD Countries: A Comparative Analysis*. OECD Social, Employment and Migration Working Papers, No. 189. Paris: OECD Publishing. https://doi.org/10.1787/5jlz9h56dvq7-en
- **URL:** https://ideas.repec.org/p/oec/elsaab/189-en.html
- **Finding:** A task-based approach finds an average of 9% of jobs automatable across 21 OECD countries (Korea 6%, Austria 12%). Occupation-level methods "might lead to an overestimation."
- **Relevance:** A methodological caution for the paper. Exposure is task-level, not job-level, which **supports the "augments" half of T1.**

## 11. Branko Milanovic

### 11a. Global Inequality (2016): VERIFIED
- **Citation:** Milanovic, B. (2016). *Global Inequality: A New Approach for the Age of Globalization*. Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard University Press. ISBN 9780674737136 **[ISBN unverified; oeaw record lists 9780674969797, likely the e-book]**.
- **URL:** https://milescorak.com/2016/05/18/the-winners-and-losers-of-globalization-branko-milanovics-new-book-on-inequality-answers-two- (Corak review); *American Prospect* 27(2), 2016.
- **Finding:** The "elephant curve" shows real income growth by 1988 global-income percentile over 1988–2008. The gains went to an emerging global middle class (largely China) and the global top 1%. Lower- and middle-income groups in rich countries form the "big dip," with little or no gain.
- **Relevance:** **Supports T2** for the globalisation era, which overlaps the internet era. The dip is the rich-country constituency whose physical-economy jobs were offshored or automated.

## Cross-cutting notes for the paper

1. **Evidence that exposure has inverted (T1):** Frey & Osborne (2013/2017) put the risk on low-wage, low-education work. IMF (2024) finds exposure rising with income (60% in advanced economies, 26% in low-income countries). Pairing the two is a compact empirical anchor for T1.
2. **Physical output unchanged, physical employment not (T1/T2):** Rodrik (2016, 2022) shows that the physical economy has already been losing labour intensity through labour-saving technology. State T1 as unchanged *output and function*, with labour already thinned.
3. **The crowding-in risk to T3/T4:** Rajan (2026) and Acemoglu & Johnson (2024) both warn that displaced workers entering low-barrier services depress wages. The paper's unmet-demand claim should be specific to credential- and capital-constrained physical sectors (care, trades, housing construction, energy transition), where Autor (2024), Garnaut (2019/2022) and Piketty's housing critique (Rognlie) point to binding supply constraints.
4. **The policy evidence on conversion (T4):** Katz et al. (2022) and Card–Kluve–Weber (2018) show that conversion works when it is sectoral, credentialed, employer-linked and financed through a 2–3 year lag. Autor's nurse-practitioner case shows scope-of-practice reform as a credential lever. Banerjee & Duflo and Rajan (2019) qualify mobility-based remedies: people stay put, so place-based delivery matters.
5. **AGI caveat:** Korinek & Suh (2024) define the boundary condition. T1–T3 hold in the slow-automation branch. If AI extends to physical tasks at scale, the physical economy stops being a refuge.
