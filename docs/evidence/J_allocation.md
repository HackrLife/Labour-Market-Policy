# J. Allocation of talent and capital toward communication/media software, c.1995–2025

Compiled 24 October 2026 from WebSearch/WebFetch only. Numbers in `J_series.csv`. "Verified" means the figure was read on the cited page. "Secondary" means it was read in a report of the source, not in the source itself.

## Verdict on the thesis

The evidence supports the thesis in part.

- **Supported.** Venture capital has been concentrated in software and internet companies for 25 years. That concentration reached 83% of US VC in 2000 and 86–89% (AI) in 2026. Energy, construction and real-estate technology have received low single-digit shares. VC as a financing model is poorly suited to physical-world technology (Gaddy et al. 2017; Lerner & Nanda 2020). Construction productivity has stagnated or fallen. Research productivity has declined across semiconductors, agriculture and medicine. Elite graduates still go mainly to software and finance (MIT 2025: 42.7% ICT and 20.5% finance, against 3.2% energy). Innovation-sector job growth is highly concentrated by place (more than 90% in five US metros, 2005–2017). Tech booms are associated with local rent increases.
- **Weak or contradicted.**
  1. The best-identified estimate of aggregate talent allocation (Hsieh et al. 2019) finds a large *improvement*, worth 20–40% of US growth per person from 1960 to 2010. The improvement came from reduced discrimination by race and sex, not from sector choice.
  2. The literature on "misallocation of talent to unproductive sectors" is mainly about finance and rent-seeking (Murphy, Shleifer & Vishny 1991; Philippon & Reshef 2012). It does not concern software. Software and IT are counted as productive in most of this literature, and they are the sector associated with the 1995–2004 US productivity revival (Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen 2012).
  3. The causal link from tech wealth to housing unaffordability is secondary. The stronger evidence points to supply restriction (Glaeser & Gyourko 2018; Kendall & Tulip 2018) and to the long decline in real interest rates (Miles & Monro 2021; Saunders & Tulip 2019). In the Gyourko, Mayer & Sinai (2013) framework, tech wealth enters only as high-income demand meeting inelastic supply.
  4. The most-cited estimate of the output cost of housing constraints (Hsieh & Moretti 2019) did not replicate (Greaney 2026).
  5. "Physical-world under-investment" is not universal. Health and pharmaceuticals receive large R&D and VC flows: pharma R&D was $116bn in 2022, against $50bn for software publishers, and global healthcare/biotech VC was $71.7bn in 2025. The cost of solar power fell about 90% between 2010 and 2023.

The defensible version of the thesis is therefore narrower. *Risk capital*, and to a lesser degree elite graduate labour, clustered in software and internet businesses whose returns scaled through advertising and network effects. Sectors with physical scale, long gestation and regulatory exposure (construction, energy deployment, housing) were left to sources of finance that grew more slowly. The resulting productivity problems in those sectors have been documented independently. Whether this clustering *caused* stagnation, rather than responding to it (regulation, the ideas production function), cannot be established from current evidence.

## 1. Murphy, Shleifer & Vishny (1991)

- Murphy, K. M., Shleifer, A., & Vishny, R. W. (1991). "The Allocation of Talent: Implications for Growth." *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 106(2): 503–530. JSTOR handle 10.2307/2937945. Also NBER WP 3530. https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v106y1991i2p503-530..html
- Abstract (verified): the most talented people organise production by others. When they start firms they "innovate and foster growth", and when they become rent seekers they "only redistribute wealth and reduce growth." The empirical result is that countries with more engineering college majors grow faster, and countries with more law concentrators grow more slowly.
- **Relevance.** The framework is the theoretical anchor for the thesis. Applying it requires arguing that consumer-internet and ad-tech work is closer to rent-seeking or zero-sum attention competition than to entrepreneurship. MSV themselves would classify it as entrepreneurship. The argument therefore needs evidence that ad-tech competes for a fixed pool of attention or ad budgets (business stealing). It is not enough to show that the sector is large.

## 2. Hsieh, Hurst, Jones & Klenow (2019)

- Hsieh, C.-T., Hurst, E., Jones, C. I., & Klenow, P. J. (2019). "The Allocation of Talent and U.S. Economic Growth." *Econometrica* 87(5): 1439–1474. DOI 10.3982/ECTA11427. https://econometricsociety.org/doi/10.3982/ECTA11427
- Verified: "between 20% and 40% of growth in aggregate market output per person can be explained by the improved allocation of talent" (1960–2010). White men held 94% of doctor and lawyer positions in 1960 and 62% in 2010.
- **Relevance.** This is contradicting evidence on the aggregate. Over the thesis period, talent allocation improved substantially along the dimension that is best measured.

## 3. Finance, talent and graduate destinations

**Philippon & Reshef (2012).** "Wages and Human Capital in the U.S. Finance Industry: 1909–2006." *QJE* 127(4): 1551–1609. DOI 10.1093/qje/qjs030. NBER WP 14644. https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/tphilipp/papers/pr_qje2012.pdf
- Verified: the excess relative wage in finance was 42% in 1933, −3% in 1980 and 51% in 2005. The education-adjusted premium averaged about 50% in 2006. The abstract states that rents accounted for "30% to 50%" of the wage differential between finance and the rest of the private sector in the recent period.

**Pian Shu.** Two papers, both verified on the NBER conference server:
- (a) "Are the 'Best and Brightest' Going into Finance? Career Choice and Skill Development of MIT Graduates," 20 March 2014. https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f71769/f71769.pdf
  - Covers MIT bachelor's graduates 2006–2012. 336 of 3,952 graduates with valid outcomes entered finance (about 8.5%, derived).
  - Higher GPA predicts *lower* entry into finance.
  - Normalised GPA was 0.01 for finance entrants and 0.47 for graduate-school entrants.
- (b) "Innovating in Science and Engineering or 'Cashing In' on Wall Street? Evidence on Elite STEM Talent," HBS working paper, November 2016. https://conference.nber.org/conf_papers/f90981/f90981.pdf
  - 8.8% of MIT S&E majors in the 2006–08 classes took a first job in finance, which made finance the single most popular industry. The share was 5.3% for the 2009 cohort, about 40% lower.
  - A one-SD increase in GPA reduces the probability of entering finance by 3.9 percentage points (−44.3%).
  - 13.4% of S&E majors from 1994–2008 became inventors.
  - **Relevance.** This qualifies the thesis. The very top performers disproportionately did *not* go to finance.
- Published version: not verified (see "Could not verify").

**MIT Graduating Student Survey 2025.** Bachelor's graduates with accepted positions, n=185. https://ir.mit.edu/projects/student-placement
- Information/Computer Technology 42.7%; Finance & Insurance 20.5%; Professional/Scientific/Technical incl. consulting 12.4%; Health care/pharma/devices 5.9%; Energy & utilities 3.2%; Chemicals/materials 2.2%.
- An undated older MIT facts page (2011–12 recruiting context) lists undergraduate employment sectors as computer technologies 22%, consulting 17%, finance 9%, other engineering 14%, energy/utilities 6%. Source: https://web.mit.edu/communications/dev/facts/alum.html
- The two sources use different category schemes, so comparing them over time is only indicative. Read together, they suggest the software share roughly doubled while the energy share halved.

## 4. Bloom, Jones, Van Reenen & Webb (2020)

- "Are Ideas Getting Harder to Find?" *American Economic Review* 110(4): 1104–44. DOI 10.1257/aer.20180338. https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/aer.20180338
- Verified from the paper PDF:
  - **Aggregate US.** Effective researchers rose by a factor of about 23 since the 1930s (4.3% a year), while research productivity fell by a factor of 41 (about 5.1% a year).
  - **Moore's law.** The research effort required rose by a factor of 18 between 1971 and 2014. Research productivity fell 6.8% a year.
  - **Agriculture.** Research productivity fell 3.7% a year (1970–2007).
  - **Medicine.** For new molecular entities it fell 3.5% a year.
  - **Firms.** Compustat firms show a decline of about 9% a year.
- **Relevance, cutting both ways.** The decline holds *in every sector, including semiconductors*. This points to a general ideas production function, not to a shortfall in physical-sector talent. It also undercuts the claim that moving talent from software into the physical economy would by itself restore growth.

## 6. Construction productivity

- **Goolsbee, A., & Syverson, C. (2023).** "The Strange and Awful Path of Productivity in the U.S. Construction Sector." NBER WP 30845, January 2023, revised February 2023. https://www.nber.org/papers/w30845
  - Published as chapter 4 in Basu, Eldridge, Haltiwanger & Strassner (eds.), *Technology, Productivity, and Economic Growth* (University of Chicago Press, NBER CRIW, 2025; ISBN 9780226839080).
  - From the BFI summary (verified):
    - Construction value added per worker was about 40% *lower* in 2020 than in 1970, while aggregate productivity roughly doubled.
    - Construction averaged 4.3% of GDP (1950–2020).
    - If construction productivity had grown 1% a year, aggregate labour productivity growth would have been about 0.18 percentage points higher each year, leaving the level about 10% higher.
    - Physical measures (houses or square feet per employee) are flat or falling.
    - More productive states do *not* gain market share.
  - https://bfi.uchicago.edu/insight/research-summary/the-strange-and-awful-path-of-productivity-in-the-us-construction-sector/
- **McKinsey Global Institute (27 Feb 2017).** "Reinventing Construction: A Route to Higher Productivity." Verified figures:
  - Construction labour productivity grew about 1% a year over the previous two decades, against 2.8% for the world economy and 3.6% for manufacturing.
  - Closing the gap would be worth about $1.6 trillion, or 2% of global GDP.
  - https://www.mckinsey.com/mgi/our-research/reinventing-construction-through-a-productivity-revolution
- **Relevance.** These sources document the physical-sector failure. Neither attributes it to a shortage of talent. Goolsbee and Syverson point toward regulation, fragmentation and missing reallocation, which is consistent with the Abundance argument.

## 7. Abundance and "It's Time to Build"

- **Klein, E., & Thompson, D. (2025).** *Abundance.* Avid Reader Press (Simon & Schuster). Published 18 March 2025, 304 pp., ISBN 9781668023488.
  - Argument: stalled progress on housing, infrastructure and clean energy reflects a post-1970s politics that prioritised process and vetoes over building.
  - Critics include Moyn (*NYT*), Teachout (*Washington Monthly*) and Kazis (*Guardian*).
  - **Relevance.** The book's causal story is *regulatory*, not a story of talent misallocation.
- **Andreessen, Marc (18 April 2020).** "It's Time to Build," a16z. https://a16z.com/2020/04/18/its-time-to-build/ Verified short quotes:
  - "We chose not to build."
  - "We can't build nearly enough housing in our cities with surging economic potential."
  - "The things we don't, like housing, schools, and hospitals, skyrocket in price."
  - **Relevance.** The essay concedes the problem from inside venture capital. Critics noted Andreessen's later local opposition to housing in Atherton (Reason, 16 Aug 2022), which is useful as evidence of a revealed-preference gap.

## 10. Superstar cities and housing

- **Gyourko, J., Mayer, C., & Sinai, T. (2013).** "Superstar Cities." *AEJ: Economic Policy* 5(4): 167–199. DOI 10.1257/pol.5.4.167. NBER WP 12355 (2006).
  - Mechanism: inelastic land supply in unique places combined with national growth in high-income households. This produces rising prices and the crowding-out of lower-income households.
- **Moretti, Enrico (2012).** *The New Geography of Jobs.* Houghton Mifflin Harcourt.
  - Each new high-tech job creates five additional local jobs (MIT Sloan Management Review summary; secondary).
- **Atkinson, Muro & Whiton (Brookings/ITIF, 9 Dec 2019).** "The Case for Growth Centers."
  - Boston, San Francisco, San Jose, Seattle and San Diego accounted for more than 90% of US innovation-sector job growth in 2005–2017.
- **US Census Bureau / Zillow (30 Apr 2019).**
  - Seattle metro rents rose 17% in 2011–2015, linked to the "Amazon effect."
  - After the Facebook IPO (2012–13), home values in areas where Facebook employees lived rose 21%, against 17% elsewhere in the Bay Area.
  - This is descriptive, not causal. https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2019/04/impact-of-tech-boom-on-housing.html
- **Hsieh, C.-T., & Moretti, E. (2019).** "Housing Constraints and Spatial Misallocation." *AEJ: Macroeconomics* 11(2): 1–39. DOI 10.1257/mac.20170388.
  - Claim: constraints "lowered aggregate US growth by 36 percent from 1964 to 2009." Relaxing constraints in New York, San Francisco and San Jose would raise GDP by 3.7%.
  - **Caveats.**
    - (i) In 2021 the authors acknowledged arithmetic errors in the text that *understated* their effects (Caplan, Econlib, 5 Apr 2021).
    - (ii) **Greaney, Brian (2026).** "Housing Constraints and Spatial Misallocation: Comment." *AEJ: Macroeconomics* 18(2): 409–28. DOI 10.1257/mac.20230141.
      - Greaney cannot replicate the result and traces it to coding errors. As originally specified, the counterfactual *lowers* output.
      - With the unit-dependence fixed, the gain is "about two orders of magnitude smaller" than 3.7%.
      - **Do not use H–M headline numbers** without this caveat. The ~0.04% in the CSV is an indicative reading of "two orders of magnitude," not a reported figure.
- **Australia.**
  - **Grattan Institute.** Kelly & Donegan (20 Jul 2014), *Mapping Australia's Economy*.
    - The Sydney and Melbourne CBDs produced almost 10% of national economic activity ($118bn) on 7.1 km² (2011–12).
    - The Sydney CBD produced 23% of Greater Sydney's output with 13% of its workforce.
    - In some outer Melbourne growth areas, only 10% of jobs are reachable within a 45-minute drive.
  - **RBA.** Kendall & Tulip (Mar 2018), RDP 2018-03.
    - Zoning raised detached house prices above marginal cost in 2016 by 73% in Sydney, 69% in Melbourne, 42% in Brisbane and 54% in Perth.
  - **RBA.** Jenner & Tulip (Aug 2020), RDP 2020-04.
    - The gap between apartment prices and costs is $355k (68%) in Sydney and $97k (20%) in Melbourne. The supply elasticity is 0.07.
  - **RBA.** Saunders & Tulip (Mar 2019), RDP 2019-01.
    - Low interest rates "explain much of" the recent rise in prices and construction.
  - **Relevance.** The Australian evidence attributes high prices to regulation and interest rates. Knowledge-job concentration matters only through the demand channel. I found no Australian study that isolates *tech* employment as a driver of house prices.

## 12. Contradicting evidence

1. **Allocation improved in aggregate.** Hsieh et al. (2019) attribute 20–40% of growth per person to better talent allocation over 1960–2010.
2. **IT spilled over into physical and traditional sectors.**
   - Bloom, Sadun & Van Reenen (2012), *AER* 102(1): 167–201: the post-1995 US productivity acceleration was concentrated in sectors that use IT *intensively*, which are largely non-tech, and is linked to IT-complementary management.
   - Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson (2021), *AEJ: Macro* 13(1): 333–72, DOI 10.1257/mac.20180386: once intangibles are counted, the TFP level is 15.9% above measured by end-2017. Software intangibles account for most of the gap. Even so, the post-2004 slowdown does not disappear (adjusted 0.71% a year against 0.40% measured).
3. **Physical-sector innovation did happen and was partly financed by markets.** The global utility-scale solar PV LCOE fell from $0.460/kWh (2010) to $0.044/kWh (2023), about 90% (IRENA via pv magazine, 27 Sep 2024). Pharma R&D ($116bn in 2022) is more than double that of software publishers. Healthcare/biotech was the second-largest VC sector globally in 2025.
4. **Housing is mainly a supply-regulation and interest-rate story.**
   - Glaeser & Gyourko (2018), *JEP* 32(1), DOI 10.1257/jep.32.1.3: a regulatory "tax" in many markets exceeds plausible externalities.
   - Miles & Monro (BoE SWP 837, Dec 2019; *Economic Policy* 2021): the fall in the real risk-free rate "more than" accounts for the 1985–2018 rise in UK house prices relative to income. Falling yields alone account for 109% under a model with a supply response, against an observed 153%.
   - In Australia: Kendall & Tulip (2018); Saunders & Tulip (2019).
   - UK and Australian house prices rose without a Silicon Valley, which weakens tech wealth as a general cause.
5. **The ideas production function.** Research productivity falls in *all* sectors, semiconductors included (Bloom et al. 2020). Adding talent to physical sectors faces the same diminishing returns.
6. **Shu (2016).** The top academic performers at MIT were *less* likely to go to finance, so the "best and brightest" claim is weaker than the rhetoric suggests.
7. **Gen-AI exposure.** Eloundou et al. ("GPTs are GPTs," arXiv 2303.10130) find that about 80% of US workers have at least 10% of their tasks exposed and about 19% have at least 50% exposed, with exposure "not limited to industries with higher recent productivity growth." This is consistent with the claim that AI automates the digital/white-collar layer. It does not show that AI automates *specifically* what the consumer-internet era built.
