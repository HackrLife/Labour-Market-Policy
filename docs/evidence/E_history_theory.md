## 1. Long-run US employment shares by sector

### 1a. Agriculture
- **Citation:** Dimitri, C., Effland, A. and Conklin, N. (2005). *The 20th Century Transformation of U.S. Agriculture and Farm Policy*. USDA Economic Research Service, Economic Information Bulletin No. 3 (EIB-3), June 2005.
- **URL:** https://primary.ers.usda.gov/webdocs/publications/44197/13566_eib3_1_.pdf
- **Findings:** 41% of the workforce was employed in agriculture in 1900, 21.5% in 1930, 16% in 1945, 4% of the employed labour force in 1970 and 1.9% in 2000. The number of farms fell 63% after 1900. No 1950 value is stated in the text.
- **Gap:** The bulletin gives no current-year (2020s) farm employment share. The ERS Chart of Note fetched (https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/98755) reports only the composition of farm labour hours (59% operator/family, 41% hired, 2014–18), not farm employment as a share of total employment.

### 1b. Manufacturing (BLS CES)
- **Citation:** Harris, K. (2020). "Forty years of falling manufacturing employment." *Beyond the Numbers* 9(16), U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, November 2020.
- **URL:** https://www.bls.gov/opub/btn/volume-9/forty-years-of-falling-manufacturing-employment.htm
- **Findings:** Manufacturing's share of total nonfarm employment peaked at 32% in May 1953 (excluding the World War II build-up). It was 22% in June 1979, when the employment level reached its all-time peak of 19.6 million (19,553 thousand), and 9% in June 2019.
- **Updated levels (FRED mirror of BLS CES, seasonally adjusted, thousands):**
  - MANEMP (https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/MANEMP): Jan 1953 16,067; Jun 1979 19,553; Jan 2010 11,447; Dec 2025 12,580; Sep 2026 12,652.
  - PAYEMS (https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/PAYEMS): Jan 1953 50,144; Jun 1979 90,108; Dec 2025 158,432; Sep 2026 159,044.
  - **Derived share:** 32.0% (Jan 1953), 21.7% (Jun 1979), 7.9% (Dec 2025), 8.0% (Sep 2026). Between the June 1979 peak and September 2026 the level fell by 6.90 million (35.3%), while total nonfarm employment rose by 68.9 million.
- **Broad sector shares:** BLS *TED* (2024), "A look at a long-term trend for the Bureau's birthday" (https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2024/a-look-at-a-long-term-trend-for-the-bureaus-birthday.htm). The goods-producing share (mining, construction, manufacturing) of nonfarm employment peaked at 44.0% in 1943 and was 13.8% in 2023. The service-providing share was 86.2% in 2023.

### 1c. Health care and social assistance (BLS CES series CES6562000001)
- **URL:** https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/CES6562000001.txt (BLS CES via FRED; SA, thousands)
- **Levels:** Jan 1990 9,116.0; Jan 2000 12,749.5; Jan 2010 16,714.7; Jan 2020 20,724.7; Dec 2024 22,901.4; Dec 2025 23,587.5; Sep 2026 23,958.6.
- **Derived:** From Jan 1990 to Dec 2025 the sector added 14.47 million jobs (+159%). Total nonfarm employment rose 45% over the same period (109,196 to 158,432). Health care and social assistance now employs roughly 1.9 times as many people as manufacturing (23.96m vs 12.65m, Sep 2026).
- **Context:** BLS *TED*, "Health care added 55,000 jobs in July 2025" (https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2025/health-care-added-55000-jobs-in-july-2025.htm). URL found but not fetched.

## 3. Size of the information/ICT sector vs market value
- **BLS CES Information sector (USINFO, SA, thousands; https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/USINFO):**
  - Levels: Jan 1990 2,671; Jan 2000 3,552; peak 3,718 in Mar 2001; Jan 2020 2,901; Dec 2025 2,842; Sep 2026 2,739.
  - **Derived share of nonfarm employment:** 2.4% (1990), 2.7% (2000), 1.8% (Dec 2025), 1.7% (Sep 2026).
  - The level in Sep 2026 was 26% below its March 2001 peak and equal to its Jan 2010 level.
  - **Caveat:** NAICS 51 (publishing incl. software publishers, telecoms, data processing/hosting, broadcasting, film) excludes computer systems design and related services, which sits in NAICS 5415, professional and business services. A broader "tech" definition would be larger. Either way, the employment share stays in the low single digits.
- **S&P 500 market-cap weights:** as of 30 Sep 2026, Information Technology was 39.7% and Communication Services 9.9%, a combined **49.6%**. Source: Schwab Center for Financial Research / S&P Dow Jones Indices (https://www.schwabassetmanagement.com/node/29586). Cross-check: the ChartRow estimate as of 22 Oct 2026 gives IT 37.8%, Communication Services 9.2% and Health Care 9.8% (unofficial; https://chartrow.com/sp500/sector-weights).
- **Contrast (derived):** About 50% of S&P 500 market value sits in IT and Communication Services, while the CES Information sector holds about 1.7–1.8% of US nonfarm payroll jobs. In the other direction, health care and social assistance holds about 15% of payroll jobs, and the Health Care GICS sector is about 10% of index weight. These are different classifications (GICS for firms, NAICS for establishments). Index weights also include foreign revenue and employees, so the comparison is illustrative rather than like-for-like.
- **EU ICT specialists (occupational measure):** 9.8 million, or 4.8% of total employment, in 2023, up 1.5 pp since 2013 (Eurostat news, 24 May 2024; https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20240524-2).
- **OECD Digital Economy Outlook 2024, Vol. 1:** I verified only growth figures. The ICT sector grew an average 6.3% a year in 2013–2023 across 27 OECD countries, about three times faster than the total economy (OECD press release, May 2024: https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2024/05/growth-of-digital-economy-outperforms-overall-growth-across-oecd.html). Telefónica's summary gives average ICT-sector growth of 7.6% in 2023. **I did not get the ICT-sector share of employment:** the PDF is robots-blocked and the HTML pages carry no body text. See "Could not verify".

## 4. Baumol cost disease
- **Baumol, W.J. and Bowen, W.G. (1966).** *Performing Arts: The Economic Dilemma; A Study of Problems Common to Theater, Opera, Music, and Dance*. New York: Twentieth Century Fund. Verified via the TAMU library catalogue: https://libcat.library.tamu.edu/Record/in00000649936
- **Baumol, W.J. (2012).** *The Cost Disease: Why Computers Get Cheaper and Health Care Doesn't*. New Haven: Yale University Press. Contributors: David de Ferranti, Monte Malach, Ariel Pablos-Méndez, Hilary Tabish, Lilian Gomory Wu. DOI 10.12987/9780300188486. https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.12987/9780300188486/html
- **Nordhaus, W.D. (2008).** "Baumol's Diseases: A Macroeconomic Perspective." *The B.E. Journal of Macroeconomics* 8(1), pp. 1–39 (De Gruyter, February 2008). Earlier version: NBER Working Paper 12218 (May 2006), DOI 10.3386/w12218. https://www.nber.org/papers/w12218
  - **Findings (paraphrased abstract):** Nordhaus uses US industry data for 1948–2001. Technologically stagnant sectors show rising relative prices and falling relative real output. Outside manufacturing, progressive sectors tend to show slower growth in hours and employment. As stagnant sectors took a growing share of the economy, aggregate productivity growth slowed. This is the mechanism behind the claim that employment shifts toward low-productivity-growth services such as health and education.

## 7. Labour-market churn (BLS JOLTS)
- **Citation:** BLS, *Job Openings and Labor Turnover — December 2025* news release, including annual 2025 data, published 13 Mar 2026. https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/jolts_03132026.htm
- **2025 annual totals (NSA):**
  - Hires: **63.0 million**, a rate of 3.3%, down 1.5m from 2024, which implies about 64.5m in 2024.
  - Total separations: **62.8 million**, a rate of 3.3%, down 251k.
  - Quits: 38.0m, 60.6% of separations, down 1.3m.
  - Layoffs and discharges: 21.2m, a rate of 1.1% and 33.8% of separations, up 1.2m.
  - Other separations: 3.5m.
- **Use:** Even a large technology-driven displacement estimate, say several million jobs over a decade, is small next to roughly 21m layoffs and discharges and 63m hires in a single year. The churn figure does not show that displaced workers are re-employed at similar wages (see section 8 and "Contradicting evidence").

## 9. Engels' pause
- **Citation:** Allen, R.C. (2009). "Engels' pause: Technical change, capital accumulation, and inequality in the British industrial revolution." *Explorations in Economic History* 46(4): 418–435, October 2009 (Elsevier; ScienceDirect PII S0014-4983(09)00019-9). RePEc: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/exehis/v46y2009i4p418-435.html. Author PDF: https://www.nuffield.ox.ac.uk/Users/Allen/engelspause.pdf
- **Findings (from author PDF):**
  - Output per worker rose 46% between 1780 and 1840, while Feinstein's real wage index rose 12%. Between 1840 and 1900, output per worker rose 90% and real wages 123%.
  - Annual growth rates, output per worker vs real wage (Table 1): 1760–1800 0.26% vs 0.39%; 1800–1830 0.63% vs 0.00%; 1830–1860 1.12% vs 0.86%; 1860–1900 1.03% vs 1.61%.
  - The profit rate roughly doubled between 1800 and 1840, rising from about 10% to above 20% by mid-century.
  - Capital's share rose from about 20% to over 40%.
  - Labour's share fell from about 60% to about one half, and from 50% in 1801 to 45% in 1841. It recovered by about 1900.
- **Relevance:** Aggregate reallocation was eventually complete and wages caught up. The transition still took about six decades of wage stagnation while productivity grew.

## Contradicting evidence (to be addressed honestly in the paper)
1. **ILO 2026:** The speed of cross-sector worker reallocation has *halved* globally over two decades, and structural transformation has slowed. The paper should not treat smooth reallocation as automatic or as fast as it was historically.
2. **Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019):** Displacement has accelerated and reinstatement through new tasks has weakened over the last three decades. Reallocation has not fully offset automation in the US.
3. **Allen (2009):** In the canonical historical case, reallocation came with roughly 60 years (c.1780–1840) of real wages lagging productivity and a sharp shift of income from labour to capital. Aggregate employment survived, but workers bore large distributional costs.
4. **Baumol/Nordhaus:** Rising employment in stagnant sectors slows aggregate productivity growth. The labour-absorbing sectors (health, education, care) are the ones that drag on measured growth and raise relative costs. This cuts against any welfare-neutral reading of reallocation.
5. **Information-sector employment is falling in levels.** USINFO is down 26% from its 2001 peak and has fallen from 2,895k (Dec 2024) to 2,739k (Sep 2026). This supports the "small employment footprint" claim. It also means the sector's productivity gains are not creating direct jobs.
6. **Housing constraints and displacement earnings losses (sections 6 and 8):** I could not verify them this session. The literature is expected to show that reallocation is impeded by geography and that displaced workers suffer persistent earnings losses. Confirm figures before citing.
7. **Classification caveat:** The CES Information sector understates "tech" employment because computer systems design sits in professional services. Use a broader definition as a robustness check.
