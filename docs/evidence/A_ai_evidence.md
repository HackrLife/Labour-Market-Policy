# A. Evidence file: AI exposure, adoption and measured labour-market effects

Compiled 24 October 2026. Every figure below was taken from a page fetched at compilation (24 October 2026) (URL given). Where the fetched page did not contain a figure, it is listed under "Could not verify" rather than stated. "Supports/complicates" notes refer to the thesis: generative AI mainly automates the digital/information layer; displacement is real but bounded; neither mass permanent unemployment nor an economy-wide productivity transformation.

## 1. Exposure estimates

### 1.1 Eloundou, Manning, Mishkin & Rock — "GPTs are GPTs"

- **Working paper:** Eloundou, T., Manning, S., Mishkin, P., & Rock, D. (2023). *GPTs are GPTs: An Early Look at the Labor Market Impact Potential of Large Language Models.* arXiv:2303.10130 (v1 17 Mar 2023; v5 21 Aug 2023). DOI 10.48550/arXiv.2303.10130. URL: https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2303.10130
- **Journal version:** Eloundou, T., Manning, S., Mishkin, P., & Rock, D. (2024). GPTs are GPTs: Labor market impact potential of LLMs. *Science*, issue 6702, pp. 1306–1308. DOI 10.1126/science.adj0998. Published 20 June 2024 (date from https://www.governance.ai/research-paper/gpts-are-gpts-labor-market-impact-potential-of-llms; issue and pages from https://citedrive.com/en/discovery/gpts-are-gpts-labor-market-impact-potential-of-llms/). Volume not shown on either page (see Could not verify).
- **Key findings (arXiv abstract):**
  - "around 80% of the U.S. workforce could have at least 10% of their work tasks affected."
  - "approximately 19% of workers may see at least 50% of their tasks impacted."
  - About 15% of tasks within an occupation are directly exposed to GPTs (α measure). With complementary LLM-powered software, the share rises to about 47–56% (ζ: 0.47 human-rated, 0.56 GPT-4-rated; Table 3, v3 PDF https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2303.10130v3).
  - Exposure is generally higher for higher-wage occupations, but it does not rise monotonically at the top. Job Zone 4 β = 0.47 (human) / 0.51 (GPT-4); Job Zone 5 β = 0.43 / 0.45 (Table 6).
  - Science summary as reported by GovAI: about 1.8% of jobs could have over half their tasks affected with simple interfaces. Including complementary software, the share is just over 46%.
- **Thesis note:** Supports the "digital/information layer" claim: exposure concentrates in high-wage cognitive and information work. It also shows that headline exposure depends on assumed complementary software (1.8% vs 46%), so the paper measures technical potential, not displacement. The authors make no adoption-timeline predictions.

### 1.2 IMF — Cazzaniga et al. (2024)

- **Citation:** Cazzaniga, M., Jaumotte, F., Li, L., Melina, G., Panton, A. J., Pizzinelli, C., Rockall, E. J., & Tavares, M. M. (2024). *Gen-AI: Artificial Intelligence and the Future of Work.* IMF Staff Discussion Note SDN/2024/001, 14 January 2024. 41 pp. DOI 10.5089/9798400262548.006. ISBN 9798400262548. URL: https://www.imf.org/en/publications/staff-discussion-notes/issues/2024/01/14/gen-ai-artificial-intelligence-and-the-future-of-work-542379 (author list on the IMF page is inconsistent about Panton). Figures were taken from the PDF copy at https://wiki.ubc.ca/images/b/bd/IMF_GenAI_Future_of_Work.pdf.
- **Key findings:**
  - Global: "About 40 percent of workers worldwide are in high-exposure occupations."
  - Advanced economies (AEs): about 60% of jobs exposed. Emerging markets (EMs): 40%. Low-income countries (LICs): 26%.
  - High exposure, high complementarity: AEs 27%, EMs 16%, LICs 8%.
  - High exposure, low complementarity: AEs 33%, EMs 24%, LICs 18%.
- **Thesis note:** The share exposed with low complementarity is the potential displacement pool: 33% of AE employment, larger than the high-complementarity share. It is still an exposure measure, not an outcome. The gradient by income level is consistent with exposure tracking the size of the information/office sector.

### 1.3 ILO — Gmyrek, Berg & Bescond (2023)

- **Citation:** Gmyrek, P., Berg, J., & Bescond, D. (2023). *Generative AI and Jobs: A global analysis of potential effects on job quantity and quality.* ILO Working Paper 96, 21 August 2023. DOI 10.54394/FHEM8239. URL: https://www.ilo.org/publications/generative-ai-and-jobs-global-analysis-potential-effects-job-quantity-and ; full text: https://webapps.ilo.org/static/english/intserv/working-papers/wp096/index.html
- **Key findings:**
  - Main effect: "likely to augment rather than automate jobs."
  - Automation potential: 5.5% of total employment in high-income countries (HICs); about 0.4% in low-income countries.
  - Augmentation potential: 13.4% in HICs, 10.4% in LICs (abstract), and 13.5% in upper-middle-income countries. The text is internally inconsistent: Section 5.3 says "up to 13 per cent" for LICs.
  - Gender (automation potential): 3.7% of female employment vs 1.4% of male employment globally. In HICs, 7.8% vs 2.9% (Section 4.2). Other sections give 7.9% vs 2.9% and 8.5% vs 3.9% (microdata).
  - Mechanism: higher female exposure reflects women's over-representation in clerical work.
- **Thesis note:** Strongly supports "bounded". The automation-exposed share is single-digit even in rich countries and concentrated in clerical (information-processing) occupations.

### 1.4 ILO — Gmyrek et al. (2025) refined index

- **Citation:** Gmyrek, P., Berg, J., Kamiński, K., Konopczyński, F., Ładna, A., Nafradi, B., Rosłaniec, K., & Troszyński, M. (2025). *Generative AI and Jobs: A Refined Global Index of Occupational Exposure.* ILO Working Paper 140, 20 May 2025. DOI 10.54394/HETP0387. URL: https://www.ilo.org/node/706766 ; PDF: https://www.developmentaid.org/api/frontend/cms/file/2025/05/WP140_web.pdf ; brief: https://www.ilo.org/node/706776
- **Key findings:**
  - "Globally, one in four workers are in an occupation with some GenAI exposure." The conclusion gives 24%.
  - Highest exposure (Gradient 4): 3.3% of global employment. Gradients 1–3 combined: 20.5%.
  - HICs: 34% of employment in one of the four exposure gradients, vs 11% in LICs.
  - Gender, Gradient 4: 4.7% of female vs 2.4% of male employment globally. In HICs, 9.6% female vs 3.5% male.
  - Mean automation score fell slightly from 0.30 (2023) to 0.29 (2025), and the SD fell from 0.30 to 0.14. Scores rose for some media and web occupations because of voice, image and video capabilities.
  - "Transformation of jobs is the most likely impact." The 2025 paper does not report a separate automation-vs-augmentation employment split.
- **Thesis note:** Supports "bounded" (3.3% in the top gradient) and "digital layer" (media/web and clerical occupations). The gender skew means the bounded displacement is distributionally concentrated.

## 2. Macro / productivity estimates

### 2.1 Acemoglu — "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI"

- **Citation:** Acemoglu, D. (2025). The simple macroeconomics of AI. *Economic Policy*. DOI 10.1093/epolic/eiae042. Working paper: NBER WP 32487, May 2024, DOI 10.3386/w32487. URLs: https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/166327 ; https://www.nber.org/papers/w32487 ; PDF https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2024-05/The%20Simple%20Macroeconomics%20of%20AI.pdf
- **Key findings:**
  - "20% of US labor tasks are exposed to AI." "among all exposed tasks, 23% can be profitably performed by AI."
  - TFP: "no more than a 0.66% increase in total factor productivity (TFP) over 10 years." After adjusting for hard-to-learn tasks, it is "less than 0.53%."
  - GDP, including investment effects: "0.93% − 1.16% over the next 10 years." With a fuller investment response, the upper bound is "around 1.4% −1.56%."
- **Thesis note:** This is the central support for "not a revolution": a cumulative decade TFP gain of ≤0.66% (≈0.05–0.07 pp per year).

### 2.2 Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson — productivity paradox

- **Citation:** Brynjolfsson, E., Rock, D., & Syverson, C. (2017). *Artificial Intelligence and the Modern Productivity Paradox: A Clash of Expectations and Statistics.* NBER Working Paper 24001, November 2017. DOI 10.3386/w24001. URL: https://nber.org/papers/w24001
- **Published chapter:** Chapter 1 in Agrawal, A., Gans, J., & Goldfarb, A. (Eds.), *The Economics of Artificial Intelligence: An Agenda*. University of Chicago Press. ISBN 9780226613338. The NBER chapter page (https://www.nber.org/chapters/c14007) gives 2018 in its citation text and May 2019 / ©2019 as the publication date. Cite as 2019 (published volume), with the WP as 2017.
- **Key argument:** Measured productivity growth roughly halved over the prior decade. Four explanations are offered (false hopes, mismeasurement, redistribution, implementation lags), and implementation lags are argued to be the largest. AI as a general-purpose technology needs complementary intangible investment, so gains may lag or be mismeasured.
- **Thesis note:** Complicates "not a revolution": absence of measured gains now is consistent with a lagged J-curve. The thesis should frame its claim as conditional on the observed horizon.

## 3. Measured labour-market effects

### 3.1 Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen — "Canaries in the Coal Mine?"

- **Citation:** Brynjolfsson, E., Chandar, B., & Chen, R. (2025, revised 2026). *Canaries in the Coal Mine? Six Facts about the Recent Employment Effects of Artificial Intelligence.* Stanford Digital Economy Lab / SIEPR Working Paper, August 2025; revised 12 August 2026. URL: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/ ; SIEPR: https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/working-paper/canaries-coal-mine-six-facts-about-recent-employment-effects-artificial
- **Data:** ADP payroll records.
- **Key findings:**
  - Original (August 2025): "early-career workers (ages 22-25) in the most AI-exposed occupations have experienced a 13 percent relative decline in employment," controlling for firm-level shocks.
  - February 2026 note: about 16% by October 2025, with no reversal. With firm-time fixed effects, the relative decline becomes notable only in 2024. Interest-rate exposure does not explain it. Source: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canaries-interest-rates-and-timinga-more-on-recent-drivers-of-employment-changes-for-young-workers
  - August 2026 revision: employment of 22–25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations "now stands 19% below where it would be had it kept pace with that of their less-exposed peers"; the gap has "widened steadily."
  - Mechanism: reduced hiring, not increased separations. Adjustment is through employment, not base pay.
  - Declines are concentrated where AI use substitutes for tasks (automation). Where it complements workers, employment is flat or rising. Older workers show no comparable gap.
  - "no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement."
  - Robust to excluding tech firms and computer occupations, and to interest-rate and remote-work controls. The effect attenuates with education controls, some trends predate generative AI, and the effect is larger in ADP than in national survey benchmarks. The authors call these "early, descriptive indicators", not causal estimates.
- **Thesis note:** This is the strongest evidence that displacement is real. It is concentrated (young workers, automation-type uses, exposed occupations) and works through a hiring margin, which fits "real but bounded". The growing gap (13% → 16% → 19%) cuts against "bounded" if it continues.

### 3.2 Humlum & Vestergaard — Denmark

- **Citation:** Humlum, A., & Vestergaard, E. (2025). *Large Language Models, Small Labor Market Effects.* Becker Friedman Institute Working Paper 2025-56, 9 May 2025. URL: https://bfi.uchicago.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/BFI_WP_2025-56-3.pdf. Also NBER Working Paper 33777. The RePEc listing (https://ideas.repec.org/p/nbr/nberwo/33777.html) now gives the title *Still Waters, Rapid Currents: Early Labor Market Transformation under Generative AI*.
- **Data:** Danish adoption surveys linked to administrative records; difference-in-differences.
- **Key findings (BFI version):**
  - "no significant impact on earnings or recorded hours in any occupation". Confidence intervals rule out "effects larger than 1%." The later NBER/RePEc version rules out effects larger than 2% two years after ChatGPT's launch.
  - Average reported time savings are 3% (2.8% of work hours in the body text).
  - "only 3–7% of workers' productivity gains are passed through to higher earnings."
  - Employer encouragement raises take-up from 47% to 83%.
  - Chatbots created new job tasks for 8.4% of workers (introduction). The body text reports new workloads for 17% of users.
  - Later version: employers respond mainly by reorganising tasks, and some adopters move into higher-paying occupations, though not enough to shift average earnings.
- **Thesis note:** Strong support for "neither doom nor revolution": precise nulls on earnings and hours, small time savings, low pass-through, and offsetting task creation.

### 3.3 Yale Budget Lab — Gimbel et al. (2025) and updates

- **Citation:** Gimbel, M., Kinder, M., Kendall, J., & Lee, M. (2025). *Evaluating the Impact of AI on the Labor Market: Current State of Affairs.* The Budget Lab at Yale (with Brookings Metro), 1 October 2025. URL: https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/evaluating-impact-ai-labor-market-current-state-affairs
- **Key findings:**
  - Occupational-mix dissimilarity: during the internet era, the 2002 mix was at most about 7 pp different from 1996. The post-November-2022 change is on a path only about 1 pp higher than at the turn of the century, "although not markedly so." Shifts were already underway in 2021.
  - The largest shifts are in Information, Financial Activities, and Professional and Business Services, attributed to pre-ChatGPT trends.
  - Shares of workers in the lowest, middle and highest OpenAI-exposure groups are stable at about 29%, 46% and 18%. Unemployed workers' occupations had about 25–35% of tasks exposed, with no upward trend.
  - Recent vs older graduate dissimilarity has stayed in the 30–33% range since January 2021.
  - "the broader labor market has not experienced a discernible disruption."
- **Updates:**
  - Budget Lab, *Tracking the Impact of AI on the Labor Market*, published 16 July 2026, updated 15 September 2026. It "does not provide clear evidence of labor market disruption associated with AI." URL: https://budgetlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/page_to_pdf/1419/publication_1419.pdf
  - Nunn, R. (2026, 7 May). *AI Is Probably Not (Yet) the Reason for Labor Market Weakening.* US unemployment rose to 4.3% in March 2026 from 3.4% in April 2023, and payroll growth averaged about 20,000 jobs per month over the prior year. URL: https://budgetlab.yale.edu/node/1495/pdf
- **Thesis note:** Supports "no doom" at the aggregate level. The "not (yet)" framing and a weakening macro labour market complicate attribution in both directions.

### 3.4 Anthropic — Massenkoff & McCrory (2026), observed exposure

- **Citation:** Massenkoff, M., & McCrory, P. (2026, 5 March). *Labor market impacts of AI: A new measure and early evidence.* Anthropic. URL: https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts
- **Key findings:**
  - "Observed exposure" combines Eloundou et al. feasibility with actual Claude usage. Coverage: Computer & Math 33% of tasks; computer programmers 75%; customer service representatives and data entry keyers 67%. About 30% of workers have zero coverage.
  - A 10 pp increase in coverage is associated with 0.6 pp lower BLS-projected employment growth (2024–2034).
  - No systematic increase in unemployment for highly exposed workers since late 2022. The design could detect differences of about 1 pp.
  - Ages 22–25: unemployment in exposed occupations is flat. The job-finding rate into the most exposed occupations fell by about 0.5 pp per month, an average 14% drop relative to 2022 that is barely significant. There is no decrease for workers over 25.
  - Top-exposure-quartile workers earn 47% more on average. They are 17.4% graduate-degree holders, vs 4.5% in the unexposed group, and 16 pp more likely to be female.
- **Thesis note:** Independent corroboration of a hiring-margin effect for young workers without an unemployment effect. Exposure is concentrated in high-wage information work.

### 3.5 Hosseini & Lichtinger — seniority-biased change

- **Citation:** Hosseini, S. M., & Lichtinger, G. (2025). *Generative AI as Seniority-Biased Technological Change: Evidence from U.S. Résumé and Job Posting Data.* SSRN 5425555. First version 31 August 2025; version fetched 27 October 2025. URL: https://www.alejandrobarros.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/ssrn-5425555.pdf. A May 2026 version exists, but it could not be read.
- **Key findings:** At adopting firms, junior employment fell relative to non-adopters by about 9% six quarters after diffusion (DiD), about 10% (triple-difference), and about 8% after eight quarters (event study). Adopters hired about 5.0 fewer juniors per quarter after 2023Q1. Senior employment "remains largely unchanged." Adoption is proxied by "GenAI integrator" postings, so the estimates are associative.
- **Thesis note:** Corroborates the entry-level hiring channel at firm level.

### 3.6 Chen, Kane, Kozlowski, Kunievsky & Evans (2025) — US CPS

- **Citation:** Chen, D., Kane, C., Kozlowski, A., Kunievsky, N., & Evans, J. A. (2025, 22 September). *The (Short-Term) Effects of Large Language Models on Unemployment and Earnings.* arXiv:2509.15510. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.15510. The fetched summary spelled one surname "Kuniesvky"; check against the PDF.
- **Key findings:** Synthetic DiD: highly exposed occupations saw weekly earnings gains of about $89 (2010 dollars) and an unemployment effect of 0.2 pp (≈0). Naive DiD gives about $96 and 1.2 pp, but the authors reject these because of pre-trend violations.
- **Thesis note:** Supports "no doom". It mildly complicates "no revolution" for exposed occupations, which show earnings gains.

### 3.7 Normann (2026) — Germany

- **Citation:** Normann, M. G. (2026). Drei Jahre ChatGPT: Auswirkungen von LLMs auf den deutschen Arbeitsmarkt. *Wirtschaftsdienst*, 106(4), 301–302. DOI 10.2478/wd-2026-0073. URL: https://www.wirtschaftsdienst.eu/inhalt/jahr/2026/heft/4/beitrag/drei-jahre-chatgpt-auswirkungen-von-llms-auf-den-deutschen-arbeitsmarkt.html
- **Key findings (qualitative; charts only):** Registered unemployment rose similarly in low- and high-exposure occupations, with no significant difference. Job seekers in high-exposure occupations increased significantly, especially in 2025. High-exposure vacancies rose and then fell, but multivariate analysis does not confirm the decline.
- **Thesis note:** Supports "no doom" and shows an anticipatory job-search response.

## 4. Adoption

### 4.1 Bick, Blandin & Deming — "The Rapid Adoption of Generative AI"

- **Citation:** Bick, A., Blandin, A., & Deming, D. J. (2024; rev. 2025). *The Rapid Adoption of Generative AI.* NBER Working Paper 32966, September 2024, revised February 2025. URL: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w32966/w32966.pdf
- **Key findings:**
  - "As of late 2024, nearly 40 percent of the U.S. population age 18-64 uses generative AI."
  - 23% of employed respondents used generative AI for work in the previous week, and 9% used it every workday.
  - 1–5% of all work hours are assisted by generative AI. Time savings equal 1.4% of total work hours, implying a potential aggregate productivity gain of 1.1%.
  - Work adoption was about as fast as the PC (genAI about 27% in 2024 vs about 25% workplace computer use in 1984). Overall adoption was faster than PCs or the internet.
- **2026 update (Real-Time Population Survey, St. Louis Fed FRED Blog, 27 Aug 2026, "Does generative AI save time at work?"):** https://fred.stlouisfed.org/blog/2026/08/does-generative-ai-save-time-at-work/
  - Q3 2024 → Q2 2026: past-week work use rose from 28.2% to 39.2% of employed adults. Work hours assisted rose from 4.1% to 6.3%. Work hours saved rose from 1.6% to 2.2%.
- **St. Louis Fed (Cancelada, 8 Oct 2025):** users saved about 5.4% of work hours (≈2.2 h per 40-h week). URL: https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2025/oct/generative-ai-productivity-future-work
- **Thesis note:** Rapid individual adoption combined with small hours saved (2.2% of all work hours by Q2 2026) supports "broad use, shallow aggregate effect."

### 4.2 US Census Bureau — BTOS

- **Latest official reading:** Grundy, A., Breaux, C., & Khatiwoda, D. (2026, 26 May). *Large Firms With at Least 20 Employees Biggest AI Users.* U.S. Census Bureau. URL: https://census.gov/library/stories/2026/05/ai-use-businesses.html
  - Current use, December 14, 2025 to May 3, 2026: "hovered between 17% and 20%". It was 19.8% nationally for the period ending 3 May 2026. Expected use within six months: 20–23%.
  - Firms with 250+ employees: 37%. Firms with 100–249: 32%. Firms with ≤4 employees: under 20%.
  - By sector: Information 39.7%, Finance & Insurance 33.9%, Retail about 14%.
  - **Question change:** the question was revised in November 2025 (from 17 Nov 2025) from AI used "in producing goods or services" to AI used "in any business function". Pre- and post-revision levels are not comparable.
- **Trend under the old "producing goods or services" wording:**
  - Census Research Matters (Dinlersoz & Goldschlag, 3 Dec 2024), September 2023 to August 2024: firms with 250+ employees rose from 5.2% to 7.8%, and firms with 1–4 from 4.6% to 5.8%. On linear trends, firms with 250+ employees would take about six years to reach 25%. URL: https://www.census.gov/newsroom/blogs/research-matters/2024/12/ai-use-small-businesses.html
  - Minneapolis Fed (Garcia Luna, 29 May 2026): about 10% of businesses in late 2025, double mid-2024. About 20% in early 2026 under the new wording. URL: https://www.minneapolisfed.org/article/2026/ai-adoption-in-business-grows-steadily-but-unevenly
  - Federal Reserve FEDS Note (Allen, 3 Apr 2026, *Monitoring AI Adoption in the US Economy*): BTOS about 18% at end-2025, compared with worker-level genAI work use of about 41% (RPS, Nov 2025) and employment-weighted AI access of about 78% (Atlanta Fed SBU; 54% for LLMs). The firm adoption share grew 68% (3.9 pp) in the year before the revision. URL: https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/monitoring-ai-adoption-in-the-u-s-economy-20260403.html
- **Function-level depth:** Bonney, K., Breaux, C. L., Dinlersoz, E., Foster, L. S., Haltiwanger, J. C., & Pande, A. A. (2026). *The Microstructure of AI Diffusion: Evidence from Firms, Business Functions, and Worker Tasks.* NBER WP 35141, April 2026. URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w35141
  - 18% of firms used AI in at least one function (32% employment-weighted); 22% expected within six months.
  - 57% of adopters use AI in three or fewer functions. The most common functions are Sales & Marketing 52%, Strategy 45% and IT 41%. 65% of adopters restrict worker use to three or fewer tasks.
- **Thesis note:** Firm adoption is about one-fifth, shallow (few functions, few tasks) and concentrated in Information and Finance. This supports "digital layer" and "not economy-wide".

### 4.3 Anthropic Economic Index (2026)

- **Economic primitives (15 Jan 2026), Anthropic Economics team:** https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-economic-index-january-2026-report
  - Claude.ai, November 2025: augmentation 52% and automation 45%. About three-quarters of API interactions are automation.
  - Computer/math tasks were 34% of Claude.ai conversations (peak 40%, March 2025) and 46% of API traffic. The top 10 tasks account for 24% of Claude.ai traffic and 32% of API traffic.
  - The top five US states account for 50% of usage but 38% of the working-age population. A 1% increase in GDP per capita is associated with about 0.7% higher usage per capita.
  - Implied productivity: a baseline of 1.8 pp per year of US labour productivity growth over a decade, about 1.0 pp after adjusting for task success.
- **Cadences (26 Jun 2026):** Massenkoff, Lyubich, Sacher, Hitzig, Zhang, Heller & McCrory. https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report
  - Code and technical work is about one-sixth of conversations. Personal use is about 35% on weekdays and just under 50% on weekends.
  - Survey of about 9,700 linked users (not representative): over a third expect AI to do most or nearly all of their tasks within a year, and 10% rate losing their own job as likely.
- **Thesis note:** Usage concentrates in software and information tasks, which supports "digital layer". The 1.0–1.8 pp per year productivity extrapolation conflicts sharply with Acemoglu (see Contradicting evidence).

### 4.4 Stanford HAI AI Index 2026 — Economy chapter

- **Citation:** Stanford HAI (2026). *Artificial Intelligence Index Report 2026*, Economy chapter. Takeaways article dated 13 April 2026. URL: https://hai.stanford.edu/ai-index/2026-ai-index-report/economy
- **Key findings:**
  - Organisational AI adoption: 55% (2023) → 88% (2025). Generative AI reached 53% adoption within three years.
  - Headcounts fell for ages 22–25 among software developers and customer support agents (2021–2025), while most older groups grew.
  - Global corporate AI investment: $581.69B in 2025 (private $344.66B). US AI investment: $285.88B.
  - US genAI consumer surplus rose from $112B to $172B between 2025 and 2026.
- **Thesis note:** Survey-based organisational adoption (88%) is far above BTOS (≈18–20%), a gap driven by large-firm sampling (see the FEDS Note). The investment scale suggests a possible J-curve.

## 5. Task-level productivity experiments

### 5.1 Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond — "Generative AI at Work"

- **Citation:** Brynjolfsson, E., Li, D., & Raymond, L. (2025). Generative AI at Work. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 140(2), 889–942. DOI 10.1093/qje/qjae044. WP: NBER 31161 (April 2023, rev. November 2023). URL: https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161
- **Key findings:**
  - NBER abstract: productivity (issues resolved per hour) up "14% on average," with a "34% improvement for novice and low-skilled workers."
  - QJE abstract (via https://academicnewsletter.sufe.edu.cn/info/344659): 5,172 agents; "15% on average." The most experienced and highest-skilled workers see "small gains in speed and small declines in quality."
  - Note the version difference: 14% (NBER WP) vs 15% (published QJE). Cite 15% for the QJE.
- **Thesis note:** Large within-task gains in a digital, text-based service task, with skill compression. Supports "digital layer"; the effect is task-level, not economy-wide.

### 5.2 Noy & Zhang (2023)

- **Citation:** Noy, S., & Zhang, W. (2023). Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative artificial intelligence. *Science*, 187–192, 14 July 2023. DOI 10.1126/science.adh2586. URLs: https://economics.mit.edu/news/study-finds-chatgpt-boosts-worker-productivity-some-writing-tasks ; https://scale.stanford.edu/publications/experimental-evidence-productivity-effects-generative-artificial-intelligence
- **Key findings (Science version):** 453 college-educated professionals. Time fell 40% and quality rose 18%. Treated workers were 2× as likely to use ChatGPT in their real job two weeks later and 1.6× two months later.
- **Working-paper version (March 2023, https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Noy_Zhang_1_0.pdf):** n = 444; time −0.83 SD (≈37%, 10 min); quality +0.45 SD. Correlation between first- and second-task grades fell from 0.49 to 0.25 (inequality "half-erased").
- **Thesis note:** Supports "digital layer". These are short, mid-level professional writing tasks.

### 5.3 Dell'Acqua et al. (2023) — "jagged frontier"

- **Citation:** Dell'Acqua, F., McFowland III, E., Mollick, E., Lifshitz-Assaf, H., Kellogg, K. C., Rajendran, S., Krayer, L., Candelon, F., & Lakhani, K. R. (2023). *Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier: Field Experimental Evidence of the Effects of AI on Knowledge Worker Productivity and Quality.* Harvard Business School Working Paper 24-013, 22 September 2023. SSRN 4573321. URL: https://mitsloan.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2023-10/SSRN-id4573321.pdf
- **Key findings:**
  - 758 BCG consultants, about 7% of the firm's individual-contributor consultants.
  - Inside the frontier: 12.2% more tasks completed, 25.1% faster, and quality more than 40% higher.
  - Below-average performers improved 43% and above-average performers 17%.
  - Outside the frontier: AI users were 19 pp less likely to reach a correct solution.
- **Thesis note:** Supports "bounded". Capability is task-specific, and misapplied AI reduces performance.

## 6. Task framework and new work

### 6.1 Acemoglu & Restrepo (2019)

- **Citation:** Acemoglu, D., & Restrepo, P. (2019). Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor. *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 33(2), 3–30. DOI 10.1257/jep.33.2.3. URL: https://www.aeaweb.org/doi/10.1257/jep.33.2.3 ; figures from https://conference.nber.org/confer/2019/YSAIf19/Automation%20and%20New%20Tasks.pdf
- **Key findings:**
  - Displacement reduced labour demand by about 0.48% per year in 1947–1987 and 0.7% per year in 1987–2017. Reinstatement added 0.47% per year in 1947–1987 and only 0.35% per year in 1987–2017.
  - Task-content change reduced labour demand by 10% cumulatively over 1987–2017. In manufacturing, displacement was about 1.1% per year (≈30% cumulative).
  - Wage bill per capita grew 2.5% per year in 1947–1987 and 1.33% per year in 1987–2017.
- **Thesis note:** Provides the framework. "Bounded" depends on reinstatement (new tasks) keeping pace, and the post-1987 evidence shows it did not fully keep pace.

### 6.2 Autor, Chin, Salomons & Seegmiller (2024)

- **Citation:** Autor, D., Chin, C., Salomons, A., & Seegmiller, B. (2024). New Frontiers: The Origins and Content of New Work, 1940–2018. *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 139(3), 1399–1465. DOI 10.1093/qje/qjae008. NBER WP 30389. URL: https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/2026-04/New-Frontiers-QJE-v139%283%29-p1399-1465-Aug-2024.pdf
- **Key findings:**
  - "the majority of contemporary work as of 2018—roughly 60%—is found in new job titles added since 1940." New-work shares: 74% for professionals, 46% for production occupations.
  - Augmentation innovations predict new-title emergence: a 10% increase in augmentation exposure predicts about 1.8% (OLS) and about 2.4% (IV) faster emergence per decade. Automation exposure loses significance once augmentation is controlled for.
- **Thesis note:** Supports "no permanent mass unemployment" historically, because new work absorbs labour. The authors show that new work is driven by augmentation, which ties the outcome to the augmentation/automation mix of genAI use.

## Contradicting evidence (findings that cut against the thesis)

1. **Entry-level displacement is growing, not stabilising.** Brynjolfsson, Chandar & Chen's relative gap for 22–25-year-olds widened from 13% (Aug 2025) to about 16% (Oct 2025) to 19% (Aug 2026 revision). If the trend persists and cohorts carry it forward, "bounded" may fail on a longer horizon. Hosseini & Lichtinger find an 8–10% junior employment decline at adopting firms. The AI Index 2026 reports falling 22–25 headcounts in software development and customer support.
2. **Displacement pool in the IMF numbers is large.** 33% of AE employment is in high-exposure, low-complementarity occupations, which is a larger potential displacement base than the "3.3% Gradient 4" ILO framing implies. The thesis should explain why it privileges the ILO calibration.
3. **Productivity extrapolations far above Acemoglu.** Anthropic's January 2026 report implies about 1.0–1.8 pp per year of US labour productivity growth over a decade, against Acemoglu's ≤0.66% cumulative TFP over 10 years. Bick et al. estimate a potential 1.1% aggregate productivity gain already at 2024 usage. Hours saved rose from 1.6% to 2.2% in under two years. If the trend continues, a "transformation" outcome is not ruled out.
4. **J-curve / implementation lag.** Brynjolfsson, Rock & Syverson argue measured gains lag GPT diffusion. Null effects at two years (Humlum & Vestergaard) may therefore understate long-run effects. Record investment ($581.69B corporate AI investment in 2025) is consistent with a pending intangible-capital build-out.
5. **Adoption faster than earlier technologies.** Generative AI work use matched or exceeded PC adoption rates, and worker-level use reached 39.2% by Q2 2026. Survey-based organisational adoption is 88% (AI Index) and employment-weighted access 78% (SBU). Diffusion is not confined to a small sector in terms of use.
6. **Capability expansion beyond text.** ILO 2025 reports rising automation scores for media/web occupations from voice, image and video capabilities. Anthropic reports Claude Code sessions with higher autonomy (delegation). These blur the "information layer" boundary over time and shift use toward automation, which is the mode associated with employment declines in Canaries.
7. **Users expect large task substitution.** In Anthropic's non-representative June 2026 survey, over a third expect AI to do most or nearly all of their tasks within a year.
8. **Reinstatement has weakened historically.** Acemoglu & Restrepo find reinstatement fell from 0.47% to 0.35% per year after 1987 while displacement rose to 0.7%. Relying on new-task creation to bound displacement is therefore not guaranteed.
